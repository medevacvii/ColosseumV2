# Five lives in first-century Rome

Enslaved people, Roman soldiers, the emperor, palace guards and prisoners — historical context and character guidance for ColosseumV2.

Compare **Rome in approximately AD 50–68**, during Peter and Paul's later ministry, with **approximately AD 90**, under Domitian. These are broad settings, not identical conditions across the empire. Daily routines below are illustrative reconstructions, not surviving hour-by-hour diaries.

Companions: [culture comparison](Roman-Culture-Apostolic-Era-vs-AD90.md), [historical map guide](First-Century-Colosseum-Recreation-Guide.md).

## 1. First, define the five roles

| Role | Main distinction | Avoid this shortcut |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enslaved person | Legally unfree and subject to another person's ownership | Every enslaved person is a chained laborer, gladiator or prisoner |
| Roman soldier | A member of a particular military unit, rank and posting | Every soldier is a legionary stationed in Rome |
| Emperor, often called Caesar | The ruler at the center of imperial patronage, administration and military authority | Julius Caesar rules in the AD 60s or AD 90 |
| Palace/imperial guard | A security role involving specific units and chains of command | All armed attendants belong to one timeless palace-guard organization |
| Prisoner | A person held in custody, whose legal status and case must be specified | Every prisoner is enslaved, convicted, a gladiator or awaiting death |

Julius Caesar died in 44 BC. For your earlier Roman setting, identify Claudius before AD 54 or Nero in AD 54–68. Around AD 90, use Domitian. “Caesar” had become part of imperial naming/titulature; its exact use depends on the ruler and context. [L5]

## 2. Life as an enslaved person

### Status and variety

Enslavement could arise through birth, warfare, sale and other routes. Work ranged from household service to skilled occupations, agriculture and dangerous extraction. Some people handled literacy, accounts or household responsibilities while remaining unfree. Economic importance or proximity to a powerful household did not equal freedom. [L1]

Their lives could include coercion, physical punishment, sexual exploitation and the risk of separation through sale. Describe these realities without treating abuse as entertainment. Affection, skill, friendships and family relationships existed under severe constraints; they did not cancel the institution's violence.

### An illustrative day: household worker in Rome

| Part of day | Plausible activities | What shapes the experience |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before the household's public activity | Prepare rooms, water, clothing or food according to assigned work | Household wealth, job and supervision |
| Morning | Assist visitors, accompany an owner, shop or perform skilled work | Permission to move and individual responsibility |
| Middle of day | Continue cooking, cleaning, carrying or workshop tasks | There is no universal protected meal/rest schedule |
| Later | Prepare a meal or reception, clean, handle errands | Household events may lengthen work |
| Night | Rest when duties permit; some remain on call | Sleeping space and privacy vary |

This table is a proposed household scene, not a timetable for agricultural laborers, miners, teachers or all imperial servants.

### Money, relationships and freedom

- Some could administer a **peculium**, a fund/property allowance under the owner's authority. It was not modern independent ownership or a guaranteed path to freedom.
- Manumission could change legal status, but was not promised to everyone. Freedpeople could retain obligations to a former owner/patron.
- Enslaved partners and children were vulnerable to owners' decisions. Do not grant their relationships the same legal security as a free citizen marriage.
- A freedperson is a distinct character status, not simply an enslaved character with a different costume.

**Change by AD 90:** the institution remains. Wars, household fortunes and new ownership could transform individual lives, but a new dynasty did not free the enslaved population. Do not assume every enslaved person after AD 70 was a captive from Judaea. [L1]

**Game scene:** a fictional household worker prepares water for visitors while trying to arrange a message to a relative. Give the character knowledge, relationships and goals beyond serving the player's quest.

## 3. Life as a Roman soldier

### Choose the unit first

| Type | Broad role | Research implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Legionary | Citizen soldier in a legion | Choose a legion and actual posting |
| Auxiliary | Infantry, cavalry or specialist service outside the legions | Citizenship and recruitment depend on date/unit/person |
| Praetorian | Imperial guard soldier | Different service conditions and political setting |
| Fleet serviceman | Naval service and associated shore work | Do not copy a legionary career unchanged |

The British Museum distinguishes citizen legionaries from predominantly non-citizen auxiliaries and highlights citizenship after long auxiliary service. These career categories should not be flattened into one universal service contract. [L2]

### A plausible garrison day

1. Receive orders or attend a duty assembly.
2. Train, drill or practice weapons when assigned.
3. Maintain arms, clothing and personal equipment.
4. Perform sentry, escort, patrol or work duties.
5. Carry out construction, transport, supply or administrative tasks where assigned.
6. Prepare food, eat and manage ordinary personal needs.
7. Rest around the duty roster, including night watches.

This is an activity sequence for a fictional scene. Campaigning, travel, illness, specialist work and rank could change it completely.

Supply was a major practical concern. Vindolanda tablet 343 discusses large quantities of grain and hides in a military supply context; its editors caution that the writer's precise military/civilian role is uncertain. It is evidence from Britain, not a transcript of a palace shift in Rome. [L3]

### Rewards and constraints

Regular service could offer pay, advancement, social connections and eventual veteran benefits. It also meant discipline, separation, injury, illness and the risk of death. Pay was not the same as disposable income: deductions and expenses mattered. Do not present retirement or citizenship as automatic regardless of survival, service or discharge status. [L2]

For this period, formal marriage restrictions affected ordinary serving soldiers, yet partners and children existed. Distinguish lived family relationships from legal recognition. Exact service length, pay and benefits require a dated unit-specific reference before being placed in dialogue or a game economy.

**Change by AD 90:** the army has passed through civil war and Flavian campaigns. Loyalty, commanders, unit histories and assignments can differ even while training, supply and discipline remain familiar. Domitian's reign also brought changes to military pay; verify precise rates before quoting them. [L5, L6]

**Game scene:** a fictional soldier checks a delivery, repairs equipment and writes or dictates a message. A day spent waiting or working can be more historically representative than constant combat.

## 4. Life as the emperor — Caesar

### Power required people and work

The emperor relied on officers, advisers, secretaries, household staff and informants. His position involved public ritual, military loyalty, patronage, legal decisions and communication. He could command enormous resources without personally knowing or doing everything.

An illustrative duty-day might include:

| Activity | Purpose | Scene to build |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Receive briefings and correspondence | Learn about provinces, armies and finances | Secretaries presenting a small selection of reports |
| Hear petitions or legal business | Exercise authority and grant decisions/favors | A controlled audience with intermediaries |
| Consult advisers and officers | Manage appointments, security and policy | A small meeting rather than a modern cabinet room |
| Perform public/religious duties | Maintain status and relations with gods/people | A researched ceremony |
| Attend a spectacle or reception | Display patronage and cultivate relationships | One dated event, not a mandatory daily outing |
| Private time within a staffed household | Eat, rest and pursue individual interests | Attendants and restricted access remain present |

The order is an interpretation. An emperor on campaign, traveling or facing a crisis would have a different routine. Ancient biographies are selective and often morally charged; avoid treating hostile anecdotes as a complete personality profile.

### Nero and Domitian need different scenes

- Nero's reign belongs to the earlier chapter. His post-AD 64 palace landscape should not be copied into a pre-fire scene.
- Domitian is emperor in AD 90. His building program belongs to a different political and architectural setting. [L5]
- Domitian's monumental Palatine palace developed over AD 81–92 in the Met's account. An exactly AD 90 scene should not silently assume the entire finished AD 92 complex was already complete. Use an approximate late-Domitianic date or mark construction phasing as uncertain. [L7]
- Public presentation, senatorial tensions and military relationships require sources for the chosen year, not a generic “evil Caesar” template.

**Game scene:** a fictional petitioner waits while officials assess whether to admit them. Let access, delay and intermediaries show power without inventing a known imperial decree.

## 5. Life as a palace guard

### Distinguish the organizations

| Group | What to represent | Chronological caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Praetorian Guard | Imperial protection and associated military/security duties | Its organization and political role changed over time |
| Julio-Claudian German bodyguard | A distinct personal guard under the earlier emperors | Suetonius reports Galba disbanding it; do not carry the same unit unchanged into AD 90 [L8] |
| Other escorts or specialized attendants | Only where supported for the chosen ruler and date | Do not combine all later guard formations into one first-century unit |
| Urban cohorts and vigiles | Other forces with distinct urban duties | Not interchangeable with the emperor's personal guards |

“Palace guard” is a useful plain-language label for a scene, but it is not enough to identify a historical unit.

### An illustrative guard shift

1. Receive the post and orders from the responsible officer.
2. Check the immediate access route and learn which visitors are expected.
3. Control entry through a designated doorway or approach.
4. Escort authorized people or accompany an imperial movement when assigned.
5. Communicate incidents through the chain of command.
6. Hand over information and responsibility to the next guard.

This is a plausible staged routine, **not a recovered Roman security procedure**. Do not add modern badges, radios, clearance levels or exact passwords and claim they are historical.

### Advantages and dangers

Praetorians enjoyed a privileged military position, but proximity to the ruler also exposed them to succession politics and loyalty crises. They were not simply ornamental soldiers standing still all day. Equally, political influence did not mean every individual guard could appoint an emperor at will. [L4]

Do not give everyone an identical cinematic black uniform or permanent full battle kit indoors. Choose dress and equipment from dated evidence and the specific duty. Armor, clothing, shields and weapons varied by occasion and unit.

**Change by AD 90:** use a Flavian-period security context. The German bodyguard's dissolution is one concrete reason not to reuse Nero's entire guard roster. Later specialized imperial cavalry/bodyguard histories require their own dating before inclusion. [L8]

**Game scene:** a fictional guard must confirm an escort instruction while a household worker waits with a delivery. Both characters have responsibilities; neither needs to become a combat encounter.

## 5A. Life as a prisoner

### Custody is not one occupation or legal status

A prisoner might be accused but untried, convicted and awaiting punishment, a captive of war, or held under a particular administrative arrangement. Some were citizens, others non-citizens or already enslaved. Do not make “prisoner” synonymous with “criminal,” “slave” or “Christian.”

Roman public custody generally served detention, coercion or holding people pending proceedings/punishment, rather than the modern model of a standard fixed prison sentence. That does not mean confinement was brief or harmless: delays could be severe. Later penal developments must not automatically be copied into the first century. [L9, L10]

| Situation | How to portray it | What not to assume |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accused person awaiting proceedings | Uncertainty, escorts and dependence on case progress | That accusation establishes guilt |
| Person awaiting execution or another penalty | A specific sentence and custodial situation | That every prisoner faced death |
| Guarded residence | Restricted movement and supervision in a residential setting | That every detention occurred in a dungeon |
| War captive | A separate military/political context | That every captive had the same destination or legal outcome |
| Person condemned to arena punishment | A specific penalty requiring its own evidence | That all gladiators were condemned prisoners |

### A plausible day in close detention

1. Wait under the conditions imposed by the custodian.
2. Seek information about a hearing, transfer or decision.
3. Obtain food, water and basic necessities according to the local arrangement.
4. Receive assistance or a message if access is permitted.
5. Endure uncertainty about health, treatment and the case's outcome.

This sequence is an illustrative scene, not a universal roster. Light, ventilation, sanitation, restraints, crowding and access to visitors varied. Do not invent exact meal schedules, guard rotations or cell dimensions and present them as documented practice.

Legal status, money, personal connections and location could affect custody and access to help. Brélaz's study of Paul's provincial imprisonments emphasizes criminal procedure and patronage; it is not proof that a particular Roman prison had the exact layout or routine proposed for the game. [L10]

### Paul, Peter and the evidence

| Evidence | What it supports | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acts 16:22–40 | A narrated imprisonment at Philippi, including an inner prison and stocks | A standard cell plan for Rome or every other detention |
| Acts 24:23–27 | Paul's custody at Caesarea, some access to friends and prolonged detention in the narrative | Identical conditions throughout his later career |
| Acts 28:16, 30–31 | Guarded residence, visitors and teaching in Rome | A Colosseum cell, exact address or later execution scene |
| Later associations with the Carcer Tullianum/Mamertine Prison | An important Christian commemorative tradition, especially concerning Peter [L11] | Archaeological proof that a particular surviving chamber held Peter or Paul |

Do not merge all Paul's imprisonments into one dungeon. The **Carcer Tullianum is near the Roman Forum, not beneath the Colosseum**. Its traditional associations require a different evidence label from the specific account in Acts 28.

### Comparison with approximately AD 90

Custody, legal hierarchy and uncertainty remain useful background in both periods. The emperor, authorities, political circumstances and individual cases change. An AD 90 Christian prisoner is a possible fictional character, but their existence in your story does not establish a universal Domitianic persecution. Use the cautions in the culture-comparison document.

For your Colosseum, distinguish **temporary holding/backstage spaces** from a documented permanent public prison. Keep the larger underground prison as an explicitly fictional extension. Neither Peter nor Paul can be placed there historically: the amphitheatre opened after their traditional death dates.

### A playable prisoner scene

Create a fictional accused person awaiting a hearing, with a clear statement that guilt has not been established. Their immediate goal is to get a permitted message to someone who can help. The player can learn about uncertainty, status and access without requiring a violent escape or presenting the character's invented case as a historical event.

If showing confinement, include the person's relationships, work, hopes and account of events; the character has a life beyond their custody. Do not make graphic mistreatment mandatory for learning the history.

## 5B. Rights of Roman citizens

### Citizenship mattered, but it was not modern equality

In the first century, being a free inhabitant of the empire did **not** automatically make someone a Roman citizen. Citizenship was a legal status carrying privileges and obligations. Its practical value depended on sex, age, family position, rank, location and the case. A citizen could still be arrested, prosecuted, imprisoned or executed.

Women could be Roman citizens, although they did not enjoy the political participation of citizen men. Non-citizens could also have property, family relationships and access to legal processes: citizenship did not create an absolute divide between people with every right and people with none.

| Area | Benefit or distinction | Important limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Arrest and punishment | Citizenship could support objections to summary corporal treatment and require different procedure | Not immunity from custody, discipline or lawful punishment |
| Scourging/interrogation | Paul's citizenship prevents a proposed examination by scourging in Acts 22 | Do not turn this into a claim that no Roman citizen was ever beaten |
| Appeal | In appropriate proceedings, appeal to imperial authority could be available | Not a universal modern appeal entitlement or automatic personal audience with Caesar |
| Property and transactions | Access to citizen forms of Roman private law could matter | Non-citizens were not universally forbidden to own property or trade |
| Marriage and inheritance | Status affected legally recognized marriage, family consequences, wills and inheritance | Sex, family authority and other restrictions still mattered |
| Public life | Eligible citizen men had a distinct civic status | The first-century empire was not a modern electoral democracy; women and many other people were excluded from offices/political participation |
| Military opportunities | Citizen status generally enabled legionary recruitment | Age, fitness, recruitment decisions and other qualifications applied |

The table is a first-century orientation, not an exhaustive legal code. Republican rules, later imperial law and modern citizenship rights must not be mixed into one timeless list. For detailed property, family or courtroom scenes, obtain a dated legal reference before assigning a specific entitlement.

### Paul: three useful examples in Acts

| Passage | What happens in the narrative | What the player can learn |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acts 16:35–39 | Paul objects to the public beating and imprisonment of uncondemned Roman citizens; the magistrates become afraid and come to them | A citizen could invoke status after mistreatment; officials might still have acted wrongly |
| Acts 22:24–29 | Paul challenges the proposed scourging; the commander discusses acquired citizenship, while Paul says he was born a citizen | Citizenship could be acquired or inherited, and its disclosure could change official behavior |
| Acts 25:9–12; 26:32 | Paul appeals to Caesar and the narrative treats that appeal as affecting the case's next steps | Appeal involves procedure and continued custody, not an instant release |

Use these as specific narrative examples, not proof of every procedural detail throughout the empire. Acts does not say exactly how Paul's family originally obtained citizenship. Do not invent a military ancestor or purchase story and present it as established biography.

### How people could become citizens

- Birth under the applicable citizenship and family-status rules.
- A grant by Roman authority, to an individual or a wider group.
- Certain forms of lawful manumission, subject to restrictions. **Not every freedperson automatically acquired full Roman citizenship.**
- Qualifying military service and an appropriate grant, especially associated with auxiliary veterans. The British Museum documents citizenship as an important reward; conditions and beneficiaries need date-specific checking. [L2, L12]

Do not give all inhabitants universal citizenship in AD 60 or AD 90. Caracalla's much later grant belongs to AD 212, with its own scope and debated exceptions. [L12]

### What citizenship did not guarantee

It did not provide modern freedom of religion, universal voting, equal protection regardless of class, immunity from taxation, protection against every abusive official, or automatic access to the emperor. A poor citizen might struggle to establish status or obtain help. A wealthy non-citizen could still possess influence and resources.

Do not copy later fully developed distinctions between higher- and lower-status punishments mechanically into every first-century scene. Specify the date and relevant legal context.

### AD 60s compared with approximately AD 90

Citizenship remains important in both periods; the new dynasty does not erase the distinction. Individual grants, military careers, officials and political circumstances can change. A person's Roman citizenship and Jewish or Christian identity are separate questions: holding citizenship does not determine their religion or guarantee safety in every religious/political dispute.

### A simple Unreal learning scene

1. Create a fictional detained citizen and a fictional free non-citizen in separate cases.
2. Give each a reason for detention; avoid implying guilt merely from custody.
3. Let the player request that an officer check claimed status and the relevant orders.
4. Show different procedural possibilities without turning citizenship into an automatic Release button.
5. Add a narration stop comparing the scene with Acts 22 and Acts 25.
6. Label the encounter and dialogue as a teaching interpretation, not a recovered court record.

| Character | Likely concern in the scene | Meaningful player interaction |
| --- | --- | --- |

## 7. Peter and Paul: biblical connections and limits

| Passage | Useful theme | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Philemon | Onesimus, relationships and Paul's appeal | Do not present every detail of Onesimus's backstory as certain |
| 1 Corinthians 7:20–24 | Status, freedom and Christian calling | Not a census of enslaved conditions |
| Acts 10 | Cornelius as a centurion | A centurion is not a generic palace guard |
| Acts 28:16 | Paul's guarded residence | Does not identify the individual guard's complete career or unit |
| Philippians 1:12–13; 4:22 | Imprisonment, praetorium-related language and Caesar's household | Place of composition and interpretation of praetorium are debated; “Caesar's household” does not mean the emperor converted |
| Romans 13:1–7 | Authorities and public obligations | Interpret in context, rather than inventing direct personal dealings with Caesar |

Separate Scripture, later Christian tradition, historical inference and invented dialogue. An AD 90 character can remember apostolic teaching; Peter and Paul should not appear alive at the later Colosseum unless the experience explicitly switches to another time or fictional premise.

## 8. Character creation checklist for Unreal

1. Pick the year and place.
2. Define legal status, occupation, unit/rank if applicable, languages and relationships.
3. Assign three ordinary tasks, one personal concern and one source-backed constraint.
4. Build the small space needed for those tasks before the whole city.
5. Use period-appropriate props; review later archaeological objects before copying them.
6. Give the character a short repeatable activity sequence with interruptions for interaction.
7. Label invented names, dialogue and composite episodes as fiction.
8. Avoid identical schedules and motives for everyone in a social category.
9. Test that the player can learn the character's circumstances without requiring combat or graphic abuse.
