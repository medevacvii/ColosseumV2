# Roman government and the judicial system

A beginner's guide for ColosseumV2, focused on approximately AD 50–68 and AD 90.

**Rome was an empire retaining Republican institutions.** The emperor dominated government, but the Senate, magistrates, governors and local councils still mattered. Courts did not form one uniform modern hierarchy, and executive, military and judicial powers often overlapped.

Read alongside [Roman lives and citizens' rights](Roman-Lives-Enslaved-Soldier-Emperor-and-Guards.md) and [the AD 60s/AD 90 cultural comparison](Roman-Culture-Apostolic-Era-vs-AD90.md).

## 1. Understand the period

| Period | Political setting | Avoid mixing it with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Republic, before Augustus | Competitive aristocratic government, assemblies and elected offices, with major changes over centuries | Treating this as the exact constitution under Nero |
| Principate, including both project settings | Emperor's power alongside continuing Republican offices | A modern parliamentary monarchy or elected presidency |
| Later Roman Empire | Further administrative and legal developments | Copying a fourth-century organization into AD 60 |

In the earlier chapter, use Claudius before AD 54 or Nero in AD 54–68. In AD 90 the emperor is Domitian. The Flavian dynasty followed the upheaval of AD 68–69. This changed rulers, appointments and political relationships, without abolishing the governing framework overnight. [G1]

## 2. Who did what?

| Institution/office | Plain-language role | Important limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emperor, princeps | Central authority, military leadership, patronage, appointments and imperial decisions | Relied on staff, delegates and elite cooperation; did not personally administer every case |
| Senate | Elite governing body involved in public business, decrees and some judicial matters | Not an independent elected parliament equal to the emperor |
| Consuls | Prestigious senior magistrates; the office continued under emperors | Their imperial-era practical power differed from their Republican predecessors |
| Praetors | Magistrates associated with jurisdiction and other assigned functions | Not every praetor handled every kind of dispute |
| Provincial governors | Administration, order and significant judicial authority within a province | Titles, appointment and powers varied by province and date |
| Procurators/prefects | Officials with particular financial, administrative or military responsibilities | “Procurator” did not always mean governor; check the actual posting |
| Municipal councils/magistrates | Local civic management, resources and jurisdiction | Not simply miniature copies of central government |
| Imperial secretaries and household staff | Managed correspondence, records and access | Influence did not always come from holding a Republican-style elected office |

The Met's account of Roman administration explains that the Senate continued but lost initiative in major military and foreign affairs. Its Flavian discussion identifies taxation and administrative recruitment as important concerns. [G1, G2]

**Working model:** ask who had jurisdiction over this place, this person and this issue. Do not begin by assuming every dispute went straight to Caesar.

## 3. Rome, Italy and the provinces were not identical

In Rome, magistrates, prefects, the Senate and emperor could be relevant in different matters. In provincial cities, a mixture of local authorities and a Roman governor operated. Some communities retained particular privileges and legal traditions.

The conventional distinction between senatorial/public provinces and imperial provinces helps explain appointments, but it is not a perfectly separate pair of sovereign governments. Imperial influence extended across the system. Titles and powers need checking for the chosen year.

**For a game scene:** a market dispute in Rome, a provincial public-order case and a military disciplinary matter may involve different officials. Do not put a provincial governor in charge of all policing in the city of Rome.

## 4. Where did law come from?

| Source | Meaning | Scene implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Statutes and inherited law | Rules built up over earlier periods | Older rules could remain relevant under a new emperor |
| Senatorial measures | Senate decisions with legal significance | A decree is not a modern parliamentary bill |
| Magistrates' edicts and procedure | Statements and mechanisms shaping administration of justice | The remedy or procedural route mattered |
| Imperial decisions and communications | Emperor's judgments, instructions and responses | Do not assume every imperial reply was addressed to everyone |
| Juristic interpretation | Advice and analysis by legal specialists | A jurist is not automatically the judge in a particular case |
| Local law and custom | Community-specific practices within Roman rule | “Roman law” did not make every local arrangement identical |

This is a simplified first-century orientation. Justinian's sixth-century compilation preserves much earlier material, but a passage in it is not automatically evidence that the same wording or practice applied in AD 60.

## 5. Civil and criminal matters

**Civil/private disputes** could concern debts, property, contracts, inheritance and family status. In the classical formulary model, an initial phase before a magistrate could define the issue/remedy, followed by a decision before an appointed private judge, the iudex. That is not a universal description of all imperial litigation.

**Criminal/public matters** could involve public accusations, statutory courts in Rome, senatorial proceedings or investigation and decision by an empowered official. Imperial cognitio developed alongside other procedures rather than replacing everything at one instant. [G3, G4]

Do not represent every trial with a modern prosecutor, defense attorney, independent professional judge and twelve-person jury. Some Roman courts involved panels, but that does not establish the same jury system in a governor's hearing.

## 6. A simplified path through a criminal case

The following is a teaching sequence, not a mandatory procedure for every jurisdiction:

1. **Complaint, accusation or incident:** an authority becomes involved. An allegation is not proof of guilt.
2. **Jurisdiction check:** identify the official empowered to deal with the place, offense and accused person.
3. **Identity/status check:** establish relevant citizenship, freedom, rank and other circumstances.
4. **Custody decision:** release under an appropriate arrangement, guarded residence or closer detention may be considered; conditions vary.
5. **Hearing/inquiry:** accusations, replies, testimony, documents and legal advice may be heard as the procedure permits.
6. **Decision:** dismissal/acquittal, conviction, delay or transfer are possibilities. Do not force every case into execution.
7. **Penalty or remedy:** match it to the offense, authority, status and period.
8. **Review/appeal if available:** follow the actual mechanism rather than offering a universal automatic appeal button.

In official inquiry, investigation and decision could belong to the same office. This differs from a modern ideal of separated police, prosecutor and judiciary. A process could also be distorted by delay, influence or abuse; that does not mean every official or judgment was corrupt.

## 7. Custody, punishment and citizenship

Public custody normally served holding or coercive purposes rather than a universal modern fixed-term prison sentence. Actual detention could last a long time. [G5]

Possible sanctions across Roman practice included financial penalties, exile/banishment, corporal punishment, forced labor and death, but **no single official could apply every sanction to everyone in every circumstance**. Citizens, non-citizens, enslaved people and people of different rank could encounter different procedures or treatment.

Citizenship could provide significant procedural advantages, including objections to summary scourging and possibilities of imperial appeal. It did not prevent arrest, trial or execution. Do not describe non-citizens as possessing no legal protections at all.

Slavery, forced labor sentences, pretrial custody and gladiatorial status must remain separate concepts. A person in a cell is not automatically an enslaved arena fighter.

## 8. Appeals: a necessary distinction

The Republican **provocatio** involved appeal to the Roman people against a magistrate's action. Imperial appeals and referrals developed in a different political setting. Do not equate the two mechanically or assume that appeal was identical to a modern right to a higher court. [G6]

In Acts 25:9–12, Paul appeals to Caesar and Festus consults his council. This supports a specific narrative example of provincial proceedings moving toward imperial consideration. It does not establish that every resident could demand a personal interview with the emperor or that Paul was immediately released.

## 9. Paul's cases as a teaching sequence

| Passage/location | Authority and issue in Acts | Useful lesson |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acts 16, Philippi | Local magistrates and Paul's objection to treatment of uncondemned citizens | Local officials, citizenship and possible official misconduct |
| Acts 18:12–17, Corinth | Gallio as proconsul declines to adjudicate the dispute as presented | Jurisdiction/classification matters; not every accusation receives the requested action |
| Acts 22:24–29, Jerusalem | Military commander and proposed scourging | Military intervention is distinct from a completed civil trial |
| Acts 23:23–35 | Transfer with escort and communication to the governor | Custody, transport and correspondence connect authorities |
| Acts 24–25, Caesarea | Felix, then Festus, hearing the case | Delay, accusations, responses and official succession |
| Acts 25:9–12 | Appeal to Caesar | A specific route toward imperial consideration |
| Acts 28:16, 30–31, Rome | Guarded residence, visitors and teaching | Continued custody need not mean a public dungeon |

Acts is an ancient narrative with theological purposes, not a complete legal handbook or courtroom transcript. For historical analysis of Paul's provincial custody, compare Brélaz's specialist study. [G7]

## 10. What differs around AD 90?

Both eras remain within the Principate. Use the same broad vocabulary of emperor, Senate, governors and local government, while changing named officials, political references and dated procedures.

The Flavians pursued administrative and fiscal priorities after civil war. Under Domitian, judicial activity and political control appear in ancient biographical accounts, which must be read critically. Do not infer from a hostile portrait that every AD 90 case was decided arbitrarily. [G1, G8]

For religion-related cases, do not invent a single universally enforced AD 90 law ordering all Christians to worship Caesar. Evidence for an empire-wide Domitianic persecution is disputed, as discussed in the companion culture guide. Do not move an event of AD 95–96 into AD 90 without changing the date label.

## 11. Build one understandable judicial scene in Unreal

1. Choose a place and date: for example a fictional provincial town in AD 90.
2. Choose a limited case, such as an alleged assault, rather than combining debt, treason and religion into one proceeding.
3. Research which official has jurisdiction there. Assign a specific role/title before making a costume.
4. Define the accused person's status and the allegation separately.
5. Build a small waiting area, hearing space, clerk's workspace and custody/escort route.
6. Give the player tasks such as delivering a written complaint, locating a witness or checking an authorized transfer order.
7. Use stages: Complaint → Authority Check → Custody Decision → Hearing → Outcome → Review/Transfer if applicable.
8. Allow more than one outcome where the chosen evidence supports it. Do not make bribery the only way through.
9. Label fictional characters, dialogue and procedural simplifications.
10. Add replayable narration comparing this reconstruction with one case in Acts.

An imperial audience, provincial hearing and city prison are distinct locations. The Colosseum is not the default courthouse, and its fictional basement prison should not become the place where every Roman legal case begins.
